# Postflop: texture, range advantage and bet sizing

After this lesson you can describe a flop in a few standard words, measure which player's range the flop favors (and in what way), explain why the same player bets small on some flops and big on others, compute the stack-to-pot ratio, and work out the one bet size that, repeated on every street, puts all the chips in by the river.

## Describing a flop: board texture

**Board texture** is the shape of the community cards: what they make easy and what they make hard. Players use a short vocabulary for it. These are descriptions, not math, but each one points at something we can count.

- **High** or **low**: in this course, a high flop contains an ace, king or queen; a low flop has nothing above a 9.
- **Paired**: two of the three cards share a rank, like <Cards hand="8s8d3c" />.
- **Suits**: **rainbow** means three different suits, **two-tone** means exactly two share a suit (a flush draw is possible), **monotone** means all three share a suit (a flush is already possible with two cards of that suit).
- **Connected**: the ranks sit close together, so many two-card holdings make a straight or a straight draw, like <Cards hand="7h6h5c" />.
- **Dry** versus **wet**: a dry flop offers few draws and few ways for a hand to have improved a lot (<Cards hand="Ks7d2c" />). A wet flop offers many (<Cards hand="Jc9c8d" />).

Why care? Because texture decides whose range the flop helps. That is the next idea.

## Range advantage and nut advantage

In module 4 you computed the equity of one range against another. On a flop, that single number already tells you something: whoever has the higher range equity has the **range advantage** on this board. It is an average over every combo in the range.

An average hides the top end. A range can have lower average equity and still hold more of the very best hands. Having more of the strongest hands is called the **nut advantage** (the "nuts" is the best possible hand on a board).

To measure "the strongest hands," we need a rule. In this course a combo is **strong** if its own equity against the opponent's whole range is at least 80%. The 80% line is a convention we chose, not a law; the widget below lets you see the full split, so nothing depends on where exactly the line sits.

### The example ranges

We need two concrete ranges. This is a **single-raised pot** (one raise before the flop): the under-the-gun player (UTG) opens to 2.5bb, everyone folds to the big blind (BB), and the BB calls.

- UTG opens `22+, A2s+, K9s+, QTs+, JTs, T9s, 98s, 87s, 76s, ATo+, KQo`: 230 combos, 230 / 1,326 ≈ 17.3% of hands.
- BB calls with `22-JJ, A2s-AQs, K6s+, Q8s+, J8s+, T8s+, 97s+, 86s+, 75s+, 64s+, 53s+, 43s, ATo-AQo, KTo+, QTo+, JTo, T9o, 98o, 87o`: 356 combos ≈ 26.8%.

A dash covers everything in between: `22-JJ` means every pair from 22 up to JJ, and `ATo-AQo` means ATo, AJo and AQo. A dash range can be written in either order.

Our BB re-raises (3-bets) with QQ+ and AK, so those hands are missing from the calling range. Both ranges are example starting points in the spirit of module 6, not solver output.

On each flop below, the combo counts drop because of card removal (module 1): any combo that uses a flop card is gone. The equities are exact, not sampled. The calculation takes every pair of combos that share no card (one from each range) and deals every turn and river they leave possible: 52 − 3 flop cards − 4 hole cards = 45 unseen cards, so C(45, 2) = 990 runouts per pair. Each pair counts equally, as in module 4.

## The continuation bet, and two shapes of betting range

After the flop, the BB acts first (module 0: postflop, the first player still in the hand to the left of the button acts first). The BB usually checks, and UTG decides whether to bet. A bet on the flop by the player who made the last preflop raise is a **continuation bet**, or **c-bet**.

A betting range comes in two broad shapes:

- **Polarized**: the bets are very strong hands plus bluffs, with the medium hands checked. You met this in module 5.
- **Merged** (also called **linear**): the bets are the strongest hands *and* the medium-strength hands below them, with few or no pure bluffs.

The bet size and the shape go together. From module 5, a polarized bettor can include bluffs in a share of B / (P + 2B) of the betting range and keep a bluff-catcher indifferent. That share grows with the bet: 20% at a third of the pot, 33% at pot. A big bet lets a polarized range carry more bluffs. A medium hand, though, dislikes a big bet: the hands that call a big bet tend to be the hands that beat it, and the hands it beats fold. A small bet is the opposite: it risks little, so a medium hand can bet and still be called by worse hands, and a weak hand loses little when it gets called.

### Texture heuristics

Put the advantages and the sizes together and you get the standard starting points for c-betting. These are **heuristics**: summaries of what strong players and solvers tend to do, not results we derive here.

- **Dry, high or paired flops** (K-7-2, A-K-4, 8-8-3): the raiser's range is ahead almost everywhere, so it bets **often and small** (around a third of the pot), with a merged range.
- **Connected, wet flops** (J-9-8, 7-6-5): the caller has many strong hands and draws, so the raiser bets **less often but bigger**, with a more polarized range.
- **Monotone flops** (Q-9-4 all hearts): the raiser bets **less often and smaller**. The usual explanation: any further heart can flip which hand is ahead, so neither side wants to build a big pot with a medium hand.

The table above backs up the first two: on K-7-2 UTG has a large edge in both measures; on J-9-8 the BB does.

## Stack-to-pot ratio

The **stack-to-pot ratio** (**SPR**) is the effective stack divided by the pot at the start of the flop. It tells you how many pots are left to bet.

<Formula>SPR = effective stack ÷ pot on the flop</Formula>

<Example title="Worked example: two SPRs from 100bb stacks">

**Single-raised pot.** UTG opens to 2.5bb and the BB calls, the small blind (SB) folds. The pot is 2.5 + 2.5 + 0.5 = 5.5bb (the SB's 0.5bb stays in). Each player has 100 − 2.5 = 97.5bb left. SPR = 97.5 / 5.5 ≈ 17.7.


**3-bet pot.** The button opens to 2.5bb, the SB 3-bets to 10bb (4× the open, the out-of-position size from module 6), the BB folds and the button calls. The pot is 10 + 10 + 1 = 21bb and each stack is 90bb. SPR = 90 / 21 ≈ 4.3.


</Example>

A low SPR means one or two normal bets put everyone all-in, so a hand like top pair is often worth all the chips. A high SPR means it takes several large bets, so you need a stronger hand to want all of them in. That last sentence is a rule of thumb; the next section makes "several large bets" exact.

## Geometric bet sizing

Suppose you hold a very strong hand and want to get all the chips in by the river, betting the same fraction of the pot on each street. What fraction?

Call the pot P, the effective stack S, the number of streets left n, and the bet fraction r (bet = r × the pot at that moment, so r = B/P as in module 3). On any street, if the pot is p before the bet, the bet is r·p and the call adds another r·p, so the pot becomes

<Formula>p + 2·r·p = p·(1 + 2r)</Formula>

The pot is multiplied by (1 + 2r) each street. Notation: x^n means x multiplied by itself n times, so after n streets the pot is P·(1 + 2r)^n. If all the chips are in by then, each player has added S, and the pot is P + 2S. Set the two equal:

<Formula>P·(1 + 2r)^n = P + 2S  →  (1 + 2r)^n = 1 + 2S/P  →  r = ((1 + 2S/P)^(1/n) − 1) / 2</Formula>

The last step undoes the power: x^(1/n) means the **n-th root** of x, the number that, multiplied by itself n times, gives x. For n = 2 it is the square root, and for n = 3 the cube root. Taking the n-th root of both sides gives 1 + 2r = (1 + 2S/P)^(1/n); subtract 1 and halve.

This is **geometric sizing**: geometric because the pot grows by the same factor each street. Notice it depends only on S/P, which is the SPR.

<Example title="Worked example: pot 100, stack 300, three streets">

SPR = 300 / 100 = 3. So 1 + 2S/P = 1 + 6 = 7.

The cube root of 7 is ≈ 1.9129, so r ≈ (1.9129 − 1) / 2 ≈ 0.4565: bet about **45.6% of the pot** each street.

Flop: bet 0.4565 × 100 ≈ 45.6. The pot becomes 100 + 2 × 45.6 ≈ 191.3, and the stack 300 − 45.6 ≈ 254.4.

Turn: bet 0.4565 × 191.3 ≈ 87.3. The pot becomes ≈ 365.9, the stack ≈ 167.0.

River: bet 0.4565 × 365.9 ≈ 167.0, exactly the stack that is left. The final pot is 100 + 2 × 300 = 700.

Compare half-pot bets: 50, then 100, then the river would need 200, but only 150 is left, so the river bet is a leftover all-in of 0.375 pot. Bets of one third of the pot (33.3, then 55.6, then 92.6) leave ≈ 118.5 behind after the river. Geometric sizing is the one constant fraction that finishes exactly.


</Example>

Run the single-raised pot from above through the formula: SPR ≈ 17.7 over three streets needs r ≈ 115.8% of the pot every street. That is why most single-raised pots do not end with 100bb stacks all-in unless someone raises: normal bet sizes cannot get there. In the 3-bet pot (SPR ≈ 4.3) three streets need only ≈ 56.2% each.

Why not bet small, small, then shove? It works, but the last bet becomes a huge fraction of the pot, which gives the opponent an easy fold on the river with every hand that cannot beat a big bet. Spreading the stack evenly is the cleanest way to get it all in; treat that as a guideline, not a theorem.

## Key points

- Board texture (high/low, paired, suits, connected, dry/wet) decides which range a flop helps.
- Range advantage = higher average equity on the board. Nut advantage = more of the strongest hands. They can point in different directions.
- A polarized range (strong hands plus bluffs) pairs with big bets; a merged range (strong and medium hands) pairs with small bets.
- Texture heuristics (starting points): dry/high/paired → bet often and small; connected → less often and bigger; monotone → less often and smaller.
- SPR = effective stack ÷ flop pot. Geometric sizing r = ((1 + 2·SPR)^(1/n) − 1) / 2 gets the stacks in over n streets; at SPR 3 over three streets that is ≈ 45.6% pot.
